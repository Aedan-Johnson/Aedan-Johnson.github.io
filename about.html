The Long Version

I did not get into security because of a movie. I got into it because something broke and nobody in the house could tell me why.

The first computer I took apart was one I was supposed to be fixing. I did get it running again, eventually, and the part that stuck with me was not the hardware. It was the feeling of understanding a system that had previously just been a box that either worked or did not. That curiosity is the whole reason I picked the Computer Networking & Security program at Monroe Career & Technical Institute instead of a general schedule.

Networking came first

Security without networking felt like guessing. So I started at the bottom: addressing, subnets, switching, routing, and what a packet actually looks like when you slow it down. Working through TestOut PC Pro and the Cisco track gave me something I trust more than memorized answers, which is a mental model of where traffic goes and what should never be allowed to follow it.

CyberPatriot is what hooked me

Competition changed how I study. In CyberPatriot you get a deliberately broken Windows or Linux image and a clock, and you have to find misconfigurations, weak policies, and bad accounts before time runs out. There is no partial credit for knowing the theory. My team took second place, gold tier, at the Pennsylvania state round of CyberPatriot XVIII, and every hour of that season taught me more about real system defense than reading ever did. SkillsUSA added the troubleshooting side, where I placed second in IT Servicing at districts.

Then AI showed up

My first internship at Achyuraa put me on the machine learning side, which I did not expect to enjoy as much as I did. I prototyped a local system to detect AI hallucinations, cleaned and structured the raw datasets, then benchmarked several models against each other and charted the results so the team could actually see which approach held up. I came back the next summer to work on their customer facing chatbot, translating service information into plain language, organizing it into a structured FAQ, and writing the WordPress deployment guide that went with it.

The through line between all of it: I like systems that have to survive contact with real users. A model nobody can interpret and a firewall rule nobody documented fail for the same reason.

What I'm aiming at

Short term, I want an internship or entry role where I can touch real infrastructure: help desk, network operations, or a security team that lets a junior person near the SIEM. Long term, security engineering. I would rather be the person who hardens the environment than the one who writes the report about why it fell over.
